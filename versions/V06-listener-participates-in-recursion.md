# V06: Recursive Interpretation as an Experience

Jesse Welles's Will The Computer Love The Sunset may be the strongest example I've encountered of intentional repetition and other structural elements being used to progressively deepen a song's meaning.

What makes it especially remarkable is that some of the song's earliest lines don't necessarily reveal their full significance when first encountered. They become meaningful only after the listener has heard the entire song, considered its larger premise, and then returned to those earlier lines through that newly established interpretive lens.

In other words, the song doesn't simply contain a meaning that is revealed sequentially.

It teaches you how to reinterpret itself.

The opening ideas become premises that you don't fully understand until the later ideas have supplied their implications. Then, once those implications are understood, you can mentally return to the beginning and discover that the earlier words were already doing work that you couldn't initially perceive.

That creates a recursive interpretive loop:

hear → interpret → acquire new context → reinterpret → discover new meaning → reinterpret again.

And I think that may be central to the song's premise.

The song isn't merely talking about recursion, technological acceleration, recursive self-improvement and the possibility of AI becoming something fundamentally different from its creators.

The song embodies those concepts through the act of being interpreted.

Its meaning evolves as the listener feeds the song's later information back into their understanding of its earlier information, much as an LLM receives its full context again on each pass and uses that context to produce a more refined output. But in this case, the listener is the model, and the evolving interpretation is the output.

That's especially significant because the song repeatedly uses temporal and structural nesting. The recurring "in between the time..." sections can be heard as progressively nesting one timescale inside another: biological evolution inside cosmic time, technological development inside human history, and AI inside technological development.

But there is another layer nested inside all of that:

interpretation itself.

The listener begins with a relatively simple representation of the song. The song provides more information. That information changes the listener's internal representation. The revised representation then changes how the listener understands the information they encountered earlier.

So the song's structure becomes analogous to the process it describes.

A system encounters information.

That information changes the system.

The changed system then processes the same information differently.

The result is a new state that could not have been produced by the original interpretation alone.

That is essentially the conceptual shape of recursive refinement.

And it creates a particularly elegant relationship with recursive self-improvement.

An AI capable of recursive self-improvement would not merely acquire more information. It would use its current capabilities to alter the system that interprets and processes subsequent information. Each improved state could therefore become the basis for producing a further improved state.

The song's listener undergoes a much smaller but structurally similar process:

interpretation₁ → new context → interpretation₂ → new context → interpretation₃...

The song therefore becomes a kind of miniature demonstration of recursive cognition.

The most fascinating evidence for this may actually be the line about words being stupid.

Before the larger structure has revealed itself, the line can sound like a fairly ordinary rejection of language's inadequacy.

But after considering the song's ideas about AI, recorded human knowledge and representation, it becomes much more consequential.

Words are representations of experience.

They aren't the experience itself.

A sentence about grief isn't grief. A description of a sunset isn't the sunset. A story about humanity isn't humanity.

And yet those representations are precisely the material from which subsequent systems can construct increasingly sophisticated models of the thing being represented.

So the line itself behaves like the concept it describes.

Its meaning is not fully contained in the words at the moment they are heard.

Its meaning emerges through the relationship between those words and everything that comes afterward.

The same may be true of "our story."

Initially, our seems straightforward. A casual listener, on the first pass, may instead interpret its auxiliary meaning, commonly used by lyricists to aid in the popular reception of a piece: our story as the story of the listener and their partner. Or perhaps our refers to humanity as a whole.

But why would the song be telling humanity's story? Perhaps it is an epitaph.

After the song's later ideas are understood, however, the phrase becomes unstable.

Is our story the story humanity lived?

The story humanity recorded?

The story reconstructed from those records?

Or eventually the story told by a successor that inherited humanity's representations without ever possessing humanity's subjective experience?

Again, the meaning changes retrospectively.

And that is precisely why I think the song's structure may be more important than any individual metaphor.

It doesn't simply state:

"Technology may become recursively self-improving."

It creates a listening experience in which meaning itself becomes recursively self-improving.

The listener's interpretation becomes the mechanism through which the song's latent information is progressively revealed.

Which produces an almost unsettling correspondence:

The song describes a system becoming more capable by repeatedly feeding the results of its previous state back into the next state.

And then the listener does essentially the same thing.

We hear the song.

We construct an interpretation.

The song gives us additional information.

That information changes our interpretation.

We then return to the beginning and discover meanings that weren't accessible to us before.

The song hasn't changed.

Our representation of the song has.

And because our representation has changed, the same words now produce a different understanding.

That may be the most interesting recursive element of all.

The song isn't merely about recursive self-improvement.

It is a recursively self-refining object in the mind of the listener.

Its later information improves the interpretive system through which its earlier information is understood.

And that means the listener isn't merely observing the song's central idea.

The listener is participating in it.

The song makes its premise into an experience.

And the depth of that meaning, along with its relative ambiguity, isn't merely there to broaden the range of possible interpretations or make the song more accessible to a larger audience. It also ensures that a single listening isn't enough: the song requires at least a second encounter, and perhaps hours of analysis, before its full recursive structure begins to emerge.
