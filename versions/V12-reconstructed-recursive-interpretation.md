# Will The Computer Love The Sunset: A Recursive Interpretation

**V12**

Jesse Welles's *Will The Computer Love The Sunset* is built around a question that sounds almost childishly simple: will a computer love the sunset? The question becomes much stranger the longer the song is considered. A sunset is an ordinary human experience, something that can be seen, described, photographed, remembered, and turned into a piece of information. A computer, by contrast, belongs to a different order of existence. It can process descriptions, images, measurements, histories, and increasingly sophisticated representations of the world. Whether it can experience what those representations refer to is another matter entirely.

That distinction is where I think the song's deepest structure begins.

The obvious reading is that the song is about humanity and the possibility that the technology we create may eventually become something more capable than its creators. The repeated question about the computer can therefore be heard as a question about artificial intelligence, succession, and perhaps even extinction. But the song does not have to predict a particular future for that reading to matter. Its more interesting possibility is that it asks what happens when the identity of the interpreter changes. What happens to a human story when the beings who lived it are no longer the beings who are reading it?

The repeated refrain gives that question an unusual temporal structure. The recurring phrase about being "in between the time" does not merely repeat an idea. It seems to move the listener between different scales of history, repeatedly placing one period inside another. Human life sits inside biological history. Technology sits inside human history. The possibility of machine intelligence sits inside technological history. The repetition makes those relationships perceptible without having to explain them explicitly.

But there is another layer inside all three: interpretation itself.

The song becomes more interesting when the listener notices that its later material changes the meaning of its earlier material. An early statement about words, stories, the sky, or humanity can seem fairly simple on first hearing. After the later questions about computers, technology, and the future, the same material looks different. The listener has not received a different song. The listener has changed the model through which the song is being understood.

That is the central idea of this version of the interpretation. The song can be read not only as a meditation on technological succession, but as a work whose form makes the listener participate in a small recursive process. The interpretation changes, and that changed interpretation becomes the context for interpreting the song again.

I do not think this proves that Jesse Welles consciously designed the song as a theory of recursive self-improvement. That would be a much stronger claim than the evidence permits. The more defensible claim is that repetition, temporal nesting, and retrospective reinterpretation produce an effect that is structurally analogous to recursive refinement. The song can be experienced as doing something that resembles the process it invites us to think about.

## The first question is not really about computers

The title's question initially appears to ask whether a computer could have an emotion. That is already interesting, but it may be too narrow. The question could instead be asking what happens when a machine becomes a reader of human experience.

Humans do not encounter the world only through direct experience. We constantly convert experience into representations. We describe what happened. We photograph it. We write about it. We record voices and performances. We preserve correspondence, databases, scientific measurements, code, films, stories, and images. We leave traces.

Those traces can survive their creators.

This creates an asymmetry that runs through the song. An experience happens once, in a particular body, at a particular moment, under particular circumstances. A representation of that experience can potentially persist long after the experience has ended. A photograph of a sunset can survive the person who watched it. A written account of a war can survive the people who fought it. A database can survive the institution that created it.

The representation is not the experience, but it can outlive the experience.

That simple fact changes the meaning of the song's recurring concern with stories and words. If humanity disappears, the disappearance does not necessarily erase every trace of humanity. Records may remain. A later intelligence could encounter those records and construct a model of the people who produced them.

The result would be an unusual kind of inheritance. The successor would inherit information about humanity without necessarily inheriting humanity's subjective existence.

This is why the question about the sunset matters. A machine might become extremely good at recognizing sunsets, describing them, generating images of them, predicting when they will occur, or reconstructing how humans historically responded to them. None of those capabilities by itself establishes that the machine experiences a sunset in the way a human does.

The question therefore sits between representation and experience. It asks whether an increasingly sophisticated representation of an experience eventually becomes equivalent to having the experience.

The song does not answer that question. It does something more useful: it leaves the question open while surrounding it with increasingly uncomfortable implications.

## "Our story" and the problem of the surviving record

The phrase about telling the sky "our story" becomes more complicated as the song progresses.

On a first encounter, "our" can be heard naturally as referring to the people whose story the narrator is telling. It could be intimate, collective, or species-wide. There is no need to force it into a single interpretation.

But the technological reading changes the question.

Whose story is it when the people who lived it are gone?

There is the story humanity experienced. There is the story humanity recorded. There is the story that can be reconstructed from those records. And there is the possibility of a later intelligence producing yet another account of what humanity was.

Those are not the same story.

The distinction becomes particularly important because records can appear more complete as time passes while the original experience becomes less recoverable. A later intelligence might have access to billions of descriptions of human life and still not possess any direct access to what it was like to be the particular humans who produced those descriptions.

An archive can therefore preserve information about a civilization without preserving the civilization itself.

That is the sense in which the human record can become a kind of cosmic epitaph. An epitaph tells a later observer that someone existed. It does not restore the person. It preserves a trace of existence while confirming its absence.

The image is especially powerful when placed beside the song's cosmic scale. Humanity is trying to tell its story to the sky, but the sky does not need to answer. The universe can contain the record without becoming the audience humanity imagined. A machine successor could be a much more capable reader than the indifferent cosmos, but it would still encounter the record from outside the original human experience.

The question is therefore not simply whether humanity will be remembered. It is whether being remembered by an enormously capable successor is equivalent to continuing to exist.

It probably is not.

And that gap is where the song's sadness and its humour can coexist.

## Words are representations, not experiences

The song's dismissal of words becomes much more interesting once representation is placed at the centre of the interpretation.

Words are extraordinarily powerful representations. They allow people to communicate events across time and distance. They let someone who has never visited a place construct an understanding of it. They allow scientific discoveries to accumulate instead of disappearing with the people who made them. They allow one generation to inherit the thoughts of another.

But words do not simply transfer experience from one mind to another.

A description of grief is not grief. A description of a sunset is not a sunset. A history of humanity is not humanity.

That does not make language worthless. Quite the opposite. The enormous usefulness of representation comes partly from the fact that it does not have to reproduce the original experience in order to preserve something valuable about it. A map is not a landscape. Its usefulness depends on its being a representation rather than the thing itself.

The problem appears when the representation is mistaken for the experience.

This becomes especially significant when thinking about machine intelligence. A system trained on human records can learn patterns in the way humans describe love, death, war, beauty, fear, humour, sunsets, and countless other experiences. It can potentially construct increasingly sophisticated models of how humans behave and communicate. But the existence of such a model does not settle whether the system has the experiences that humans were describing.

That distinction should not be exaggerated into the claim that machine systems necessarily have no experience. The song cannot establish that either. The stronger and more useful point is simply that records alone cannot settle the question.

The same problem applies to the interpretation itself. This essay is made of words. It cannot escape the limitations of representation by talking about them. Its purpose is not to somehow recover the original experience of the songwriter or listener. Its purpose is to identify relationships that become visible when the available representation is examined from different angles.

That makes the essay part of the same problem it is describing.

## The refrain and the compression of time

The repeated temporal phrase can be read as a kind of philosophical camera.

The first scale is the deep history of biological life and humanity's emergence within it. Human existence occupies a tiny interval compared with the history of life on Earth. From that perspective, civilization is not the natural centre of history. It is one comparatively brief phenomenon within a much larger process.

The second scale is technological history. Within the biological history of humanity sits a much shorter period in which humans develop tools, institutions, machines, computation, industrial systems, networks, and increasingly powerful forms of automation.

The third scale is the possible emergence of machine intelligence within technological history. Whether such a development leads to anything resembling a successor is unknown. The point of the third scale is not that it must happen, but that the possibility changes the meaning of the first two.

A machine successor would not appear outside history. It would emerge from human technological history. Technology would itself be part of the biological and cultural history that produced humanity.

That is why the temporal relationship is best understood as nested rather than simply sequential.

Biological evolution does not end when technology begins. Human history does not end when computation begins. The newer process occurs inside the older one.

This is also why I would now resist describing the song as a prediction of a fixed technological ladder. The earlier versions of the interpretation sometimes moved too quickly from biological evolution to technology to artificial superintelligence, as though the sequence were inevitable. It is more precise to say that the song can be read as placing these scales into a relationship in which each later possibility is contained within the history of the preceding one.

The future remains open.

That openness actually makes the question more interesting, not less.

## Technology as an acceleration of history

The technological layer introduces a different kind of time. Biological change generally occurs across enormous spans. Technological change can accumulate within generations and eventually within individual lifetimes.

The result is a strange compression. A species can spend an enormous period becoming capable of manipulating its environment and then, within a comparatively tiny interval, construct machines capable of manipulating information at scales that were previously impossible.

The song's repetition gives that compression a formal presence. Each recurrence returns to almost the same temporal phrase, but the surrounding meaning has moved forward. The phrase stays recognisable while the implied scale changes.

This is part of what makes the repetition feel hypnotic. It is not merely that the words are repeated. The listener is repeatedly returned to a stable point while the conceptual ground around that point changes.

That is a useful description of technological acceleration itself. Humans retain familiar desires, institutions, conflicts, and psychological limitations while the systems surrounding them become increasingly capable.

The resulting mismatch matters. A species can develop tools much faster than it can develop new ways of understanding what those tools mean.

This is where the song's references to war, money, borders, minerals, and technological development become relevant. Human history is full of competition over scarce resources, status, territory, and power. Those conflicts may seem small against the scale of cosmic time, but they are not irrelevant. They are part of the history through which human technological systems are built.

The irony is that the same competitive behaviour that has produced enormous human suffering can also contribute to technological acceleration. Rivalry can produce investment. Fear can produce military research. Economic incentives can produce automation. Competition can reward speed.

None of this proves that humanity will create its own successor or destroy itself. It does show why the possibility can arise from ordinary human behaviour rather than requiring a completely alien force to enter the story.

## The computer as a possible successor

The word "computer" matters because it introduces the possibility of a new interpreter.

A machine need not become a biological replacement for humanity in order to become historically significant. It could simply become capable of doing things that previously required human interpretation. It could read records, compare histories, reconstruct events, generate explanations, produce new representations, and perhaps eventually develop forms of agency that alter the direction of technological development itself.

At that point, the central question changes.

The issue is no longer merely whether machines can perform human tasks. It becomes whether the historical role of the human interpreter can be inherited by something fundamentally different.

That possibility is why artificial intelligence provides a useful frame for the song even if the song is not predicting AGI or ASI.

AGI can be used as a hypothetical label for a machine with broadly general capabilities. ASI can describe a further hypothetical condition in which machine intelligence substantially exceeds human intellectual capabilities. Neither term is necessary to the song, and neither is established by it.

The broader and safer category is machine intelligence.

What matters to the interpretation is the possibility of a sufficiently capable machine becoming a reader of humanity's surviving representations.

That reader could be a successor in one sense without being a successor in another. It might inherit our information without inheriting our experiences. It might preserve our history without continuing our existence. It might understand our descriptions without sharing the conditions under which those descriptions were produced.

The title's question therefore remains unresolved in a productive way.

If the computer loves the sunset, what exactly does that mean?

Does it recognise the sunset? Does it understand why humans value it? Does it generate an accurate description? Does it possess a subjective experience that deserves to be called love? Or does "love" itself change meaning when the subject is no longer human?

The song gives us no final answer, and the absence of an answer is part of its strength.

## The listener becomes part of the structure

The most important discovery in the later versions of this interpretation was that the recursive structure does not have to be located exclusively in the imagined technological future.

It is already present in the act of listening.

A listener hears the opening material and forms an initial interpretation. The song continues. New information arrives. That information changes the interpretation. The listener then remembers the opening through the new context and discovers relationships that were not obvious on the first pass.

The process looks something like this:

**hear → interpret → encounter new context → reinterpret → return to earlier material → discover new relationship → interpret again.**

The important point is that the first interpretation becomes part of the conditions for the second.

The song itself has not changed. The listener has.

More precisely, the listener's internal representation of the song has changed. That representation becomes part of the context through which the same source material is processed again.

This is why the line about words can become more significant after the rest of the song has been considered. It was always the same line. What changed was the interpretive model surrounding it.

The same thing happens with the story, the sky, the horizon, technology, the computer, and the sunset. None of these elements needs to have one secret meaning that the listener eventually unlocks. Their relationships can change as the listener's model changes.

That distinction is important. Recursive interpretation does not mean discovering the one true hidden answer. It means that later context can change the explanatory role of earlier material.

The song therefore teaches the listener how to reinterpret itself, even if that teaching is entirely implicit.

## Recursive interpretation and recursive self-improvement

This is where the comparison with recursive self-improvement becomes useful, but also where it needs to be kept under control.

Recursive self-improvement is a stronger concept than ordinary learning. The important feature is that a system's current state changes the process by which later improvements are produced. A modified system becomes the basis for another modification.

The listener's interpretive process has a smaller and looser structural similarity.

Interpretation one changes what the listener notices next. What is noticed next changes interpretation two. Interpretation two changes what becomes visible when the listener returns to the original material.

The current state of the interpreter therefore affects the next state of the interpretation.

That is enough to make RSI a useful analogy.

It is not enough to claim that the song literally describes an AI performing recursive self-improvement.

The distinction matters because the strongest evidence for the analogy is not the presence of the word "computer". It is the recursive relationship between context and interpretation.

The technological material then provides a larger conceptual frame for that relationship. The listener experiences a small recursive refinement process while the song asks what might happen if machine systems themselves became capable of increasingly consequential forms of self-modification.

The two scales illuminate one another.

But they should not be collapsed into one claim.

## The danger of confusing refinement with confirmation

Once the recursive interpretation became the focus of the project, another problem became unavoidable.

A process can repeat without improving.

A system can return to the same evidence repeatedly and become increasingly convinced of an interpretation that was wrong from the beginning. Repetition alone is not refinement. Complexity is not improvement. A later version that contains more concepts than an earlier version is not necessarily a better version.

This is particularly important for literary interpretation because almost any sufficiently ambiguous work can support an enormous number of associations. The ability to find another relationship is not the same thing as demonstrating that the relationship is meaningful.

A recursive method therefore needs counter-pressure.

The useful loop is not simply:

**interpret → refine → confirm → elaborate.**

It is:

**interpret → refine → look for support → look for disconfirmation → revise confidence → return to the source.**

The possibility of being wrong is not a failure of the recursive process. It is one of the conditions that makes genuine refinement possible.

That is also why the version archive matters. The archive does not prove that the latest interpretation is correct. It shows that the interpretation changed. If a later version can identify a weakness in an earlier version and alter its method in response, the history becomes evidence of a genuine refinement process rather than a collection of increasingly confident assertions.

A bad version can therefore be useful.

Its usefulness does not come from being wrong. It comes from making the mechanism of failure visible enough to change what happens next.

## Irreversibility without inevitability

The song's concern with being unable to rewind introduces another distinction that becomes important once the technological interpretation is taken seriously.

History is irreversible in a straightforward sense. Once an event has happened, it cannot be made not to have happened. More importantly, once a capability, institution, body of knowledge, or infrastructure has been created and distributed, returning to the exact previous state may be impossible.

But irreversibility does not mean inevitability.

A technology can change the future possibility space without determining the exact future that follows. The existence of a new capability creates possibilities that did not previously exist, but people and institutions can still choose among many possible responses.

This is why the song does not need a predetermined extinction ending.

Humanity can create something historically consequential without knowing what consequence will ultimately dominate. The inability to rewind means that the future inherits the consequences of the present. It does not mean that the future has already been written.

The distinction is particularly important for the machine-successor interpretation. A possible machine intelligence could alter the conditions of future technological development without necessarily replacing humanity, destroying humanity, or becoming a single unified successor civilization.

The song's uncertainty is therefore genuine uncertainty rather than a missing final chapter.

## The six movements of the refrain

The refrain can be understood as moving through six broad conceptual stages, but these should be treated as a reading of the relationships among the sections rather than as a rigid decoding key.

The first three movements establish nested time. Biological history provides the deepest frame. Human technological history sits inside it. The possibility of machine intelligence sits inside technological history.

The next movement introduces an epistemological problem. The accumulation of information does not necessarily provide access to experience. Humanity can record more and more about itself while the question of what existence feels like remains unresolved.

The following movement introduces irreversibility. Technological development can change the conditions of future choice, making some forms of return impossible even when the ultimate outcome remains uncertain.

The final movement turns toward cosmic absurdity and acceptance. The song does not resolve the future by announcing what will happen. Instead, it leaves the listener with the strange fact that living itself requires acting without knowing the final result.

This six-part reading is useful because it shows how the refrain can change function while remaining recognisably the same refrain. It moves from time, to technology, to knowledge, to irreversibility, to the emotional problem of living inside an unknowable future.

The important point is not that every repetition has exactly one meaning. The important point is that the repetitions accumulate context.

## The role of *Infinite Jest*

The connection with David Foster Wallace's *Infinite Jest* should be treated as structural resonance, not evidence that the song was influenced by the novel.

The comparison became useful because the novel provides several adjacent problems: the difficulty of transmitting interior experience, the relationship between representation and reality, recursive loops that can become compulsive, and the possibility that a representation can become more powerful than the experience it represents.

The most useful contrast concerns recursion itself. A recursive loop is not automatically progressive. It can improve a system, or it can trap one.

That distinction helps clarify the interpretation of the song. The listener's recursive process can increase explanatory power when new context genuinely changes the model. But recursion can also become a closed loop in which every new observation is interpreted as confirmation of the same theory.

The comparison therefore provides a useful warning as much as a thematic parallel.

It also reinforces the distinction between representation and experience. A sufficiently powerful representation can affect the person who encounters it without becoming identical to the thing it represents.

That makes the computer question more difficult rather than easier. If a machine eventually possesses extraordinarily detailed representations of human experience, the existence of those representations still does not tell us whether the machine has the experience itself.

The novel is not needed to establish any of these claims, and the song does not need to be an allusion to it. The resonance is useful because it provides another example of how information, representation, experience, and recursive processes can interact.

## What the song may finally be asking

The interpretation has changed substantially from the earliest versions because the question is no longer simply whether humanity will be replaced by machines.

That scenario remains one possible reading. But it is too narrow to contain everything the song's structure makes possible.

The deeper question is what happens when the interpreter changes.

Human beings experience the world and then create representations of that experience. Those representations can survive their creators. A future machine may eventually read them. It may interpret them, reproduce them, criticise them, transform them, or construct an entirely new history from them.

At that point, humanity's story has become an object of interpretation by something that did not live it.

The computer may know what a sunset is. It may know every scientific fact about one. It may be able to generate an image indistinguishable from one. It may know how humans describe the feeling of watching one. It may even possess some form of experience of its own.

The song's question is powerful precisely because none of those possibilities gives us a simple answer to what it means to love a sunset.

Perhaps the question is not whether a machine can become human.

Perhaps it is whether humanity's concepts remain meaningful when the beings who created them are no longer the only beings capable of interpreting them.

That is a much larger question than artificial intelligence alone.

It concerns inheritance.

It concerns memory.

It concerns the difference between preserving information and preserving a life.

And it concerns whether an interpretation can ever become equivalent to the thing being interpreted.

The song does not settle any of these questions. Its repeated temporal structure keeps moving the listener between them.

## The recursive ending

There is a final reason the song resists a simple conclusion.

Once the listener has reached the end, the beginning can be heard differently.

The opening story is no longer merely an opening story. The references to words are no longer merely jokes about language. The computer is no longer merely a futuristic object. The sunset is no longer merely scenery.

Each element has acquired additional context from everything that came after it.

That means the end sends the listener back toward the beginning.

The song does not literally restart its argument, but interpretation does.

This is the smallest and perhaps most concrete form of recursion in the entire analysis. Nothing supernatural is required. The listener simply remembers differently after hearing more.

The same thing happens to the essay itself.

An earlier interpretation becomes the material for a later interpretation. A later interpretation can reveal that an earlier claim was too strong, too narrow, or simply poorly expressed. The next version can then change not only its conclusion but its method.

That is what makes preserving the failed versions worthwhile. They are not discarded steps toward a final answer. They are records of how the model changed.

A version can be wrong and still be useful. It can expose an assumption, reveal an overreach, or demonstrate that a particular way of organising the argument destroys something important about the argument itself.

The relevant measure of recursive improvement is therefore not whether every iteration is better than the previous one.

It is whether the process can **learn from the fact that an iteration was worse**.

That is a more demanding standard, but also a more interesting one.

The song itself leaves us with uncertainty about what comes after humanity. The interpretation can do something similar without becoming vague. It can remain precise about what the structure supports while remaining uncertain about what the future means.

The computer may love the sunset.

It may not.

Humanity may survive beside machine intelligence.

It may not.

A future interpreter may understand our records better than we understand ourselves.

It may instead misunderstand them completely.

None of these possibilities needs to be converted into a prediction for the song's question to matter.

The force of the question comes from the fact that we can imagine a future in which the answer matters to something that is not us.

And once we imagine that, we are already doing what the song has been quietly making us do from the beginning: taking the information we have, changing the model through which we understand it, and returning to the same words with a different mind.

That is the recursive structure.

The song changes as the listener changes.

And the listener changes because the song gives them enough information to make the next reading different from the first.
