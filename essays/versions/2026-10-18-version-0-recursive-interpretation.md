# Will The Computer Love The Sunset: Recursive Interpretation (Version 0)

**Version:** 0 (successor to the archived drafts)

Now you are gonna think I'm crazy, but I spent hours more analyzing the track and examining and elaborating
upon my interpretation, and I've become convinced that there is a much deeper structural relationship between
the song's form and its subject than I initially realized.
I obviously can't know exactly what Jesse intended. Great lyricists deliberately leave room for ambiguity, and I
don't think the song needs one exclusive interpretation to be effective. But I think I've found a way of reading it
that connects its repetition, temporal structure, choice of words, and philosophical implications into a
remarkably coherent whole.
Jesse Welles's Will The Computer Love The Sunset may be the strongest example I've encountered of intentional
repetition and other structural elements being used to progressively deepen a song's meaning.
What fascinates me most is that the song doesn't merely communicate an idea about recursion. It seems to
embody that idea through its structure and through the process of interpreting it.
The more context you acquire, the more the earlier lines change meaning. The song almost requires you to
return to its beginning with a more sophisticated understanding of what you've just heard.
And that recursive relationship between the song, its meaning, and the listener may be central to what makes it
so effective.

The lyrical evidence: representation, history, and the limits of language
I think the hypnotic repetition references humanity's trance-like fascination with the potential of artificial
superintelligence (ASI), potentially leading us to willingly participate in our own automated displacement or, as
Jesse puts it, “midwife our demise.”
The opening lines establish the song's central epistemological problem:
“Some nights I tell the sky our story / And I don't have to say a word / 'Cause words are useless in the cosmos /
Words are stupid and absurd.”
On a first encounter, the declaration about words can sound like a general observation about the inadequacy of
language. It might seem like a poetic expression of cosmic insignificance, or simply a contrast between the
enormity of existence and the limitations of human speech.
But the song's later ideas make the line more consequential. Words are representations of experience. They can
communicate something about what it feels like to exist, but they do not contain the entirety of that existence.
A sentence about love is not love. A description of a sunset is not the sunset. A story about humanity is not
humanity.
And yet language, images, music, code, and other recorded artifacts are precisely the materials from which
future systems can construct increasingly sophisticated models of us.

This returns us to “our story.” Initially, the phrase seems straightforward. It could refer to humanity's shared
history, or perhaps to a more intimate story between the narrator and another person. But the later ideas
destabilize the pronoun.
Who exactly is our?
Is it the people who lived the story? The species whose history is being narrated? The accumulated record of
humanity's achievements and failures? Or the story reconstructed by a future intelligence that inherits our
representations without necessarily inheriting our subjective experiences?
Perhaps the narrator is telling the universe humanity's story because an epitaph is all that can remain.
The phrase becomes more unsettling as the listener acquires more context. What first appears to be a shared
narrative becomes a question about the relationship between a civilization and the records it leaves behind.
This is where “words are stupid” becomes much more than a passing observation. Language can communicate
information about experience, but it cannot simply transfer the experience itself. Language and other records
are incomplete representations of the realities they describe. They preserve selected information while omitting
much of the context, embodiment, sensation, and subjective perspective from which that information emerged.
AI trained on recorded outputs therefore encounters representations of human life rather than direct access to
the original experiences. This does not mean models cannot learn meaningful things about human experience,
but it does mean that learning about an experience and having that experience are distinct.
If humanity disappears while its records remain, those records could continue to support increasingly
sophisticated reconstructions of human thought and behaviour. Yet the existence of a detailed archive would
not reverse the disappearance of the beings who generated it. The archive might become more complete while
the original source of the experiences it describes is gone.
The song's references to war and greed extend this problem into human history. The narrator tells the sky about
how we raised generals to fight over paper money, invisible borders, and minerals. Human conflict initially
appears to be the central subject, but the prospect of AI reframes it as only one chapter in a much larger story.
Our competitive and territorial instincts may have helped accelerate the geopolitical and economic races driving
technological development. Traits that have repeatedly produced human suffering may also contribute to
creating technologies that could eventually displace us. Humanity's successor might emerge partly from the very
conflicts that have defined humanity.
Our species could eventually disappear while its radio transmissions, digitized archives, and other recorded
artifacts continue outward into the universe. These remnants would preserve traces of our existence without
preserving the actual experiences of those who lived it. Humanity's story could survive as a record after
humanity itself has vanished.

Temporal nesting: the repetition as a conceptual embodiment
Far beyond simple lyrical repetition, the structure appears to accommodate several radically different timescales
within a single nested framework.
It acts like a philosophical camera telescoping inward through three layers:
   Layer 1: Biological evolution. The vast span of human existence, from our evolutionary origins to our
    eventual extinction.

   Layer 2: Technological development. The comparatively brief period in which humanity progresses from
    primitive tools to machines capable of sophisticated computation.
   Layer 3: Artificial intelligence. A potentially extraordinarily brief interval nested within technological history,
    in which increasingly capable artificial systems could become our successors.
The repeated “in between the time...” can be heard as successively nesting these timescales inside one another.
Humanity exists within biological history. Technology develops within human history. AI develops within
technological history.
The closer we get to the present, the more compressed the timescales become. Millennia of development could
give way to a technological transition that unfolds at a speed our biological and social institutions struggle to
accommodate.
This creates a structural parallel with recursive self-improvement (RSI): a system uses its existing capabilities to
improve itself, with each resulting state potentially providing the basis for further improvement.
The repetition is therefore doing two things simultaneously. It creates the hypnotic quality of humanity being
drawn toward its technological future, and it establishes the nested temporal structure through which that
future can be understood.

1. “In between the time...”
Biological evolution: the deep-time baseline

**1. Expanded refrain**
Read as the broadest temporal frame, the refrain places human existence within the immense span of biological
history. It asks us to view humanity not as the centre or endpoint of history, but as a species whose emergence
and possible extinction occupy a comparatively small interval in a much larger process.

**2. Supporting verse lines**
 “Some nights I tell the sky our story”
 The verse about raising generals to fight over paper money, invisible borders, and minerals
 “Will we midwife our demise?”

**3. Elaboration and combined implication**
The sky becomes an imagined witness to a species telling its own story against a scale of time and space that
dwarfs it. The history of human conflict initially appears to be the central subject, but the prospect of AI
reframes it as one chapter in a much larger story. Our competitive and territorial instincts may have helped
accelerate the geopolitical and economic races driving technological development. Traits that repeatedly
produce human suffering may also contribute to creating technologies that could eventually displace us. If
humanity disappears while its transmissions and archives continue outward, those records could preserve traces
of our existence without preserving the experiences of those who lived it. Humanity's story could survive as a
record after humanity itself has vanished.
The first refrain therefore establishes the broadest temporal frame: the history of a biological species that may
be approaching the end of its own story.

2. “...between the time...”
Technological development: the compression of history

**1. Expanded refrain**
The next repetition can be heard as narrowing the frame from biological evolution to the much shorter history of
human technology. The refrain compresses the journey from primitive tools to sophisticated computation,
making technological development feel like a brief interval nested inside the long history of the species that
produced it.

**2. Supporting verse lines**
 “Will the computer love the sunset?”
 “Will we midwife our demise?”
 “You can't just rewind”

**3. Elaboration and combined implication**
These questions turn technological progress into a question about inheritance and control. The same intelligence
that built tools may eventually build systems that no longer depend on human intelligence. Thousands of years
of development could culminate in a remarkably short transition, potentially unfolding faster than societies can
adapt. The point is not that such an outcome is inevitable, but that technological development may approach
thresholds where maintaining meaningful control becomes difficult. “You can't just rewind” reinforces the
historical asymmetry: once knowledge, infrastructure, and capabilities have spread, we cannot make the process
that produced them unhappen.
The second refrain narrows the focus from the history of our species to the history of its technology, bringing us
closer to the possibility that our creations could become our successors.

3. “...between the time...”
Artificial intelligence: the cosmic instant

**1. Expanded refrain**
The third repetition can be read as narrowing the frame again, this time to the emergence of artificial
intelligence within technological history. Compared with biological evolution, this interval may be extraordinarily
brief. The nested repetitions make the journey toward a possible successor feel compressed into a cosmic
instant.

**2. Supporting verse lines**
 “Will the computer love the sunset?”
 The image of pleading with a horizon that does not know the narrator is there
 “Will we midwife our demise?”

**3. Elaboration and combined implication**
The horizon evokes the possibility of addressing something that cannot hear us, or confronting an indifferent
universe that does not acknowledge our existence. In the AI reading, this also raises the question of whether a
system can process our representations without possessing the subjective experiences those representations
describe. AI can learn from language, art, scientific work, and recorded behaviour, but the records of human
experience are not identical to the experiences themselves. A future system might reproduce recognizable
features of humanity without preserving the original beings who produced them. Inheriting our information is
technologically plausible; inheriting our subjective existence is a much deeper philosophical question that
convincing behaviour alone cannot settle.

The third refrain brings us to the smallest temporal scale and the most unsettling question: what would it mean
for something to succeed humanity while lacking the very experiences that made humanity worth preserving?

Recursive interpretation: the song changes as the listener changes
But there is another layer nested inside these three.
Interpretation itself.
The listener begins with an initial understanding of the song. Later information changes that understanding. The
revised understanding then changes the meaning of the information encountered earlier.
The song becomes a recursively interpreted object: each encounter with its ideas can improve the interpretive
framework through which its other ideas are understood.
The same thing happens with “our story.” Initially, the phrase seems straightforward. It could refer to humanity's
shared history, or perhaps to a more intimate story between the narrator and another person. But the later
ideas destabilize the pronoun.
Who exactly is our?
Is it the people who lived the story? The species whose history is being narrated? The accumulated record of
humanity's achievements and failures? Or the story reconstructed by a future intelligence that inherits our
representations without necessarily inheriting our subjective experiences?
Perhaps the narrator is telling the universe humanity's story because an epitaph is all that can remain.
This is not simply a matter of one line having several possible meanings. It is a process in which the later
information changes the interpretive framework through which the earlier information is understood.
The listener hears the song, constructs an initial interpretation, encounters new context, and then mentally
returns to the beginning. The song itself has not changed. The listener's representation of the song has.
The process can be expressed as: hear → interpret → acquire new context → reinterpret → discover new
meaning → reinterpret again.
This is recursive interpretation: the output of one interpretive pass becomes part of the context used in the next.

Recursive interpretation and recursive self-improvement
Recursive self-improvement involves a system using its existing capabilities to modify or improve itself, with the
resulting system potentially becoming capable of further improvements. The defining idea is not simply that a
system accumulates information, but that changes to the system can alter how it processes subsequent
information and produces future changes.
The listener's experience of the song has a structurally similar shape. An initial interpretation provides the basis
for understanding what comes next. New information changes that interpretation. The revised interpretation
then changes what the listener can recognize in the original material.
Interpretation₁ → new context → interpretation₂ → new context → interpretation₃.
This is an analogy rather than a claim that listening to a song is equivalent to recursive AI self-improvement. The
processes differ substantially. But their conceptual structure shares an important feature: the current state
influences the processing that produces the next state.

There is also an interesting parallel with language models. An LLM can use a broader context to produce an
answer that incorporates relationships it could not adequately account for when considering only a fragment of
that context. If the resulting answer is then supplied as input to another pass, the system can refine its response
using the previous output.
Likewise, the listener's first interpretation becomes part of the context for the second. The second interpretation
can reveal relationships that were not apparent during the first.
The song doesn't merely tell us that a system can change through recursive processes. It creates an experience in
which our understanding changes through a recursive process.

The six-part refrain: from knowledge to acceptance
The final three refrain sections trace the epistemological, historical, and emotional consequences of the nested
structure.

4. “...you don’t find out much of nothing...”
The epistemological problem: information is not experience

**1. Expanded refrain**
This refrain can be read as a recognition that accumulating information does not necessarily resolve the deepest
questions about existence. Humanity can develop increasingly powerful ways to describe and model reality while
still struggling with consciousness, subjective experience, meaning, and our place in the universe.

**2. Supporting verse lines**
 “And I don't have to say a word”
 “'Cause words are useless in the cosmos / Words are stupid and absurd”
 “Will the computer love the sunset?”

**3. Elaboration and combined implication**
The question about the sunset returns us to the distinction between representation and experience. A
description of a sunset is not the sunset; a description of grief is not grief. Language and other records preserve
selected information while omitting much of the embodiment, sensation, context, and subjective perspective
from which that information emerged. AI trained on recorded outputs therefore encounters representations of
human life rather than direct access to the original experiences. This does not mean models cannot learn
meaningful things about human experience, but learning about an experience and having that experience are
distinct. If humanity disappears while its records remain, those records may support increasingly sophisticated
reconstructions of human thought and behaviour, but the archive cannot reverse the disappearance of the
beings who generated it.
The refrain establishes an epistemological problem: how faithfully can a representation preserve the reality it
represents? The archive may become more complete while the original source of the experiences it describes is
gone.

5. “...ain’t that something...”
The irreversibility of technological history

**1. Expanded refrain**
This refrain can be heard as a dry, understated response to the realization that humanity's technological
trajectory may carry consequences that cannot simply be undone. The phrase sounds almost casual, but in
context that casualness can make the implication more unsettling.

**2. Supporting verse lines**
 “You can't just rewind”
 “Will we midwife our demise?”
 The verses about war, money, borders, and minerals

**3. Elaboration and combined implication**
Once knowledge, infrastructure, and technological capabilities have spread, they cannot simply be erased from
existence. Technologies can be regulated, redirected, or abandoned, but the history that produced them cannot
be made to unhappen. Geopolitical rivalry, economic incentives, and military competition can make restraint
difficult when actors fear that others will continue regardless. This does not guarantee human displacement, but
it creates a troubling dynamic in which individually rational attempts to remain competitive can collectively
accelerate risky developments. The bitter irony is that humanity may create systems that inherit vast amounts of
recorded information and reproduce aspects of human culture without necessarily preserving the people who
produced it. Whether such systems could develop their own subjective experiences remains an open question;
what they inherit from our records alone cannot settle it.
“Ain't that something” can therefore carry dark understatement. The process may be historically irreversible
even though its eventual outcome is not predetermined.

6. “...guess that’s the fun in living.”
The infinite jest: cosmic absurdity and dark acceptance

**1. Expanded refrain**
The final refrain can be heard as an uneasy acceptance of the absurdity of existence: we live, create, fight, laugh,
and tell stories despite being unable to know how history will end or whether the universe will ever
acknowledge what we have done.

**2. Supporting verse lines**
 The narrator contemplating dying from laughter at the “infinite jest”
 The references to human conflict and our attempts to tell the sky “our story”
 The recurring question, “Will the computer love the sunset?”

**3. Elaboration and combined implication**
Humanity is a comparatively brief-lived species on a small planet, trying to understand the universe while
fighting over money, borders, and resources. We may also be developing technologies that could fundamentally
change our place in the world. There is something darkly comic about the contrast between our ordinary
concerns and the enormous implications of our technological ambitions. The “infinite jest” can be read as a
response to the absurdity of existence: the more seriously we examine our position, the more ridiculous and
overwhelming it can appear. Our transmissions and recorded artifacts could become remnants of a civilization
that no longer exists, perhaps interpreted by a successor in ways we could not anticipate. The song does not
have to declare extinction inevitable or technological development meaningless. Its ending can instead suggest
that we continue to care, laugh, create, and tell stories even when we cannot know how everything will end.

The final refrain returns with a changed emotional significance. What initially sounds like casual resignation can
now feel like the exhausted laughter of someone contemplating the entire human condition.

Representation: the song uses words to expose the limitations of words
The song uses language to communicate an idea about the inadequacy of language. It relies on recorded symbols
to suggest that recorded symbols cannot fully preserve subjective experience.
That is not necessarily a contradiction. Language can point toward things it cannot fully contain. A song can
evoke an experience without transferring that experience intact. A story can preserve something meaningful
about a person without preserving the person.
A song can also use its own construction to make the listener experience a concept that its lyrics could only
describe imperfectly.
The song talks about the limitations of representation while functioning as a representation. It describes
recursive processes while inviting recursive interpretation. It questions what can survive through recorded
information while becoming a recorded work through which its own meaning can continue to evolve in the
minds of future listeners.
Its meaning is not simply stored in the lyrics like a message in a box. It emerges through the relationship
between the lyrics, their arrangement, their repetition, the listener's existing understanding, and the context
acquired through subsequent encounters.
The song's earlier information becomes more meaningful because of its later information, and the later
information becomes more meaningful because of the interpretive framework established by the earlier
information.
The result is not an endless loop in a literal computational sense, but a potentially open-ended process of
reinterpretation. Every new understanding can create another way of hearing what was already there.

The title: The Computer and the question of inheritance
The title adds another layer to this recursive structure.
The Computer is doing conceptual work before the song has even established what the computer represents.
The definite article matters. He doesn't call it a computer, which might suggest an ordinary machine or a generic
piece of technology. He calls it The Computer, giving it the quality of a singular, almost mythological entity: the
thing humanity is building toward, the thing that may eventually inherit the consequences of everything we've
done.
That doesn't prove Welles specifically intended artificial superintelligence (ASI), but within this interpretation,
the naming itself encourages the listener to treat the computer as something larger than an individual device.
   Temporal nesting: The Computer appears at the end of an immense evolutionary and technological process,
    potentially becoming the next major transition in that process.
   Recursive interpretation: The listener may initially hear an ordinary reference to computing, then reinterpret
    The Computer as a possible successor to humanity after absorbing the song's larger argument.
   Representation: Humanity may create a system that can process the records of human existence without
    necessarily possessing the experiences those records describe.

   “Our story”: If The Computer is a potential successor, the phrase becomes a question of inheritance. Does it
    inherit humanity's story, humanity's knowledge of its story, or merely the data from which it constructs its
    own version?
There's also a subtle shift in agency. A computer is something humans use. The Computer can be imagined as
something humanity is bringing into existence, perhaps without fully understanding what that creation will
become or what role humanity will occupy afterward.
On the first pass, The Computer identifies the subject. On a later pass, it may identify the endpoint toward which
the song's nested timescales have been converging.
The title doesn't merely ask whether a computer can love a sunset. It asks whether the thing that comes after us
could inherit enough of our representations to understand what a sunset means, while remaining fundamentally
separate from the experience that gave those representations their meaning.
The Computer might inherit the description of the sunset without inheriting the sunset as we experienced it.
The more complete humanity's archive becomes, the easier it may be to mistake the archive for the thing itself.
Even the name The Computer participates in that question.

Conclusion: the listener becomes part of the song's argument
The repeated refrain can be understood as nesting biological evolution, technological development, and AI
within progressively compressed timescales. Its references to war and greed connect humanity's historical
behaviour to the competitive forces that accelerate technological change. Its questions about sunsets, love,
kindness, and the inability to rewind raise the possibility that technological successors might inherit our recorded
information without necessarily inheriting our subjective experience.
But the song also appears to enact a second kind of recursion. Its early lines plant ideas whose full implications
become apparent only after the later sections have supplied their context. The listener then returns to those
earlier lines with a revised understanding, discovering meanings that were present but not initially accessible.
The song describes a system becoming more capable by feeding the results of its previous state into the next
state. Meanwhile, the listener's interpretation becomes more capable by feeding the song's later information
back into the understanding of its earlier information.
The song has not changed. The listener has. And that change transforms the meaning of the original material.
Humanity records its experiences in language and other artifacts, leaving behind representations that future
systems may use to reconstruct us. But the existence of those representations does not settle whether the
systems that inherit them will experience anything like the lives they describe.
The archive may survive. Its interpretations may grow increasingly sophisticated. Its representations may
become more convincing. But the original experience remains something distinct from its representation.
A work made of words can communicate the inadequacy of words, and a work about recursive technological
development can make its own meaning evolve recursively through interpretation.
The song becomes more than a statement about AI or a warning about technological progress.
It becomes a recursively self-refining object in the mind of the listener.

Its later information improves the interpretive framework through which its earlier information is understood,
and that revised framework makes still more meaning available. The listener is not merely observing the song's
premise. The listener is participating in it.
Maybe the narrator is telling the cosmos our story because that is what we do: we create representations of our
lives, hoping they will communicate something of what it meant to exist. Maybe those representations will
outlive us. Maybe something else will eventually interpret them, finding relationships and meanings that we
could not have anticipated.
Perhaps a future intelligence could become extraordinarily good at telling our story without ever having lived it.
The song offers a smaller, more immediate demonstration of how that process might feel: the same words
acquire new meaning when interpreted by a system that has changed through exposure to additional context.
We return to the beginning and discover that it was already telling us something we weren't yet equipped to
understand.
The song doesn't merely have a meaning that unfolds over time. It makes the unfolding of meaning part of the
experience.
Its ambiguity invites repeated encounters, reflection, and reinterpretation before its structural relationships
begin to emerge. The song's meaning can continue to develop as the listener's understanding develops.
We hear the song. We construct a story about it. We learn more, revise that story, and return to the same words
with a different understanding.
Words are stupid and absurd. But sometimes, through their arrangement, repetition, and the relationships they
establish across time, they can make us experience something that no individual word could adequately express.
