# Retrospective foreshadowing, “our story,” and “The Computer”

This note records a later discussion that sharpens two connected interpretive claims: the opening lines function as retrospective foreshadowing, and the definite article in “The Computer” can suggest a singular successor rather than an ordinary device. These are interpretive readings, not verified statements of Jesse Welles's intent.

## 1. “Words are stupid” as retrospective foreshadowing

On first encounter, “words are stupid” can reasonably sound like a general dismissal of language: words are inadequate, clumsy, ridiculous, or small beside the cosmos. The line's more specific role becomes available only after the song develops its ideas about time, humanity, AI, and recorded knowledge.

That makes it a form of **retrospective foreshadowing**. The line appears early, before a listener can realistically derive all of its later implications. Subsequent material changes how the opening is understood, making it feel as though the song planted a premise before revealing its consequence.

The line can then be read as an epistemological warning: language represents experience but is not identical to experience. Words, records, symbols, and artifacts can preserve information about a person or civilization without preserving the subjective lives that generated them.

## 2. “Our story” as an unstable pronoun

“Our story” initially sounds ordinary and shared. It might refer to humanity as a whole, people living now, a biological lineage, the narrator and listener, or an intimate human story. Later context makes the pronoun less stable. Who is included in “our,” and who is entitled to tell or inherit the story?

Possible referents include:

- people alive now;
- humanity across history;
- the biological lineage;
- the narrator and listener;
- the accumulated cultural record;
- humanity and whatever might follow it;
- a future intelligence that identifies humanity through the record it inherits.

The ambiguity is productive because the phrase establishes a shared representation before the song has fully revealed the problems with representation itself.

The two phrases therefore work as a conceptual pair:

> “Words are stupid” questions whether language can adequately represent reality.

> “Our story” establishes a seemingly shared account of reality, one made and transmitted through language and other records.

Later ideas make their relationship visible. The story is composed of words, records, symbols, artifacts, and representations. It can preserve information about people without necessarily preserving the people themselves.

## 3. The recursive loop: experience, record, inheritance, interpretation

The discussion developed this sequence:

**experience → language → recorded story → inherited representation → interpretation → new representation**

Human beings have experiences, describe them, and leave records. A future AI system could consume an enormous accumulation of these records and construct increasingly sophisticated models of human behaviour, history, and culture. Such a model might support meaningful understanding, but the records are still representations of the experiences rather than the original experiences themselves.

This distinction should be stated carefully. It does not prove that AI cannot have subjective experience, nor does it show that a model cannot learn anything meaningful about human experience. It identifies a difference between inheriting information about a life and having lived that life. Whether a future system could possess its own subjective experience remains an open question.

The song itself is implicated in this problem: it uses words to question the limits of words. That need not be a contradiction. Language can point toward what it cannot fully contain. A song can evoke an experience without transferring that experience intact.

The structure also resembles the song's broader recursive effect. Later context changes the interpretation of earlier language; the revised interpretation then becomes a new representation of the song. The listener returns to the opening with an altered framework and discovers implications that were not reasonably available on the first pass.

## 4. “The Computer” as a possible singular successor

The definite article in “The Computer” carries interpretive weight. “A computer” might suggest an ordinary machine or a generic technology. “The Computer” can sound singular, almost mythological: the system toward which technological development is converging, or a potential successor that inherits the consequences of humanity's history.

The title does not establish that Welles specifically intended artificial superintelligence (ASI). Rather, the phrase allows that reading to develop as the listener acquires context.

This possibility connects to several other parts of the interpretation:

- **Temporal nesting:** The Computer can be imagined as a possible next transition within the compressed sequence from biological evolution to technology to AI.
- **Recursive interpretation:** a listener may first hear a reference to computing in general, then reinterpret “The Computer” as a possible successor after considering the song's larger structure.
- **Representation versus experience:** a future system might process records of human existence without those records, by themselves, establishing that it shares the experiences they describe.
- **“Our story”:** the question becomes whether the successor inherits humanity's lived history, its account of that history, or merely the data from which it constructs its own account.

The title can also shift the agency implied by the word “computer.” A computer is ordinarily something people use; The Computer can be imagined as something humanity is bringing into existence without knowing what role it might eventually occupy. This is a suggestive interpretation, not a prediction that such a successor must emerge or displace humanity.

## 5. Why the two ideas strengthen one another

The title and the opening lines form a loop of their own. The Computer may inherit the descriptions and records through which humanity attempted to explain itself. Yet the song's opening questions whether those descriptions can adequately convey the reality they represent.

The more complete humanity's archive becomes, the easier it may be to confuse the archive with the thing itself. A future system might become extraordinarily capable at reconstructing the story of humanity without that capability, on its own, resolving whether it possesses the subjective experiences described by the story.

On this reading, the song does not merely contain an AI metaphor. Its language is implicated in the problem it explores: the lyrics are a representation that invites the listener to question the limits of representation. The opening plants the premise; later context reveals its consequences; returning to the opening changes what the listener can hear there.

## Interpretive boundaries

- Treat “retrospective foreshadowing” as a description of the listener's experience of the song's structure, not proof of deliberate authorial design.
- Treat “The Computer” as a plausible ASI reading, not confirmed intent.
- Do not equate recorded information with lived experience.
- Do not infer from this distinction that machine consciousness is impossible.
- Preserve the distinction between the song's ambiguity, the user's interpretation, and any claim about what the songwriter intended.
